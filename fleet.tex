  \large Delegation and amalgamation under fog of war}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note describes the second half of the \code{starship} maze: not one
agent, but \emph{three}, orchestrated by container delegation and amalgamation. A
\emph{scout} is the only agent that can perceive; it ranges ahead, maps the maze,
retreats on contact with a monster, and reports. A \emph{captain} is blind to the
true maze --- its entire state is a fog-of-war map it assembles from scout reports
--- yet it is the piece that must reach the exit, and it does so marching only on
ground a scout has revealed. A \emph{fighter}, blind as well, is sent by the captain
to clear whatever blocks the way. The construction makes three claims precise. The
permission model is three disjoint command registries. The amalgamation is a
genuine semilattice join, not a tuple gather. And existential hiding --- the captain
reasoning over a store it cannot see past --- is literal: the captain reaches the
exit with roughly two-fifths of the maze still unknown to it. Everything is
deterministic; two runs produce the same bytes, including a self-contained replay
website the demo generates from the move trace.
\end{abstract}

\section{The one idea}

The single-agent maze (see \code{maze.tex}) proves one agent in a real
perceive--reason--act loop. It does not exercise the showpiece's tagline ---
\emph{orchestration by delegation and amalgamation} --- which until now lived only in
the scripted star-system bridge. The fleet closes that gap on the sharpest possible
example: \textbf{partial observability}. The captain acts on a map it cannot see
directly, only on what scouts report. That is existential hiding made visible rather
than asserted.

A container is a pair $(P, R)$: shapes $P$ one may ask, and for each $p$ a type
$R\,p$ of permitted answers. A morphism (an open agent) is a pair $u : P \to Q$
(control flows \emph{down}) and $f : (p:P) \to T(u\,p) \to R\,p$ (data flows
\emph{up}, contravariantly). A \emph{worker} is a stateful morphism
$\Delta S \otimes X \to Y$, where $\Delta S$ is the state object; it unfolds into a
forward map that reads state and answers, and a backward map that splits into a
writeback of new state and the answer sent up. The fleet is three workers composed:
the scout and the fighter are workers the captain spawns, and the captain is itself
a worker whose state is a fog map. Nesting is \emph{existential} hiding: the captain
sees a sub-agent's report, never its store.

\section{Three agents, three registries}

Each agent is a different sub-container of the \emph{same} command-line surface (the
\code{dungeon} CLI over the maze world). What distinguishes them is an allowlist ---
the \emph{registry} --- checked by the harness before any command runs. The registry
\emph{is} the permission grant, enforced by absence; there is no \code{bash}, no
generic ``run a command.''

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}l l l X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Agent} & \textbf{Registry} & \textbf{Perceives} & \textbf{Role} \\
\midrule
Scout   & \code{\{look, move\}}   & the true maze (its own \code{look}) & map it, retreat on contact, report \\
Fighter & \code{\{move, attack\}} & nothing --- \emph{blind}            & clear the monster the captain names \\
Captain & \code{\{move\}}         & only its \code{FogMap}              & decide, and march to the exit \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

This is the star-system's ``Science is read-only; Helm cannot fire; Weapons cannot
steer'' re-themed: the fighter cannot \code{look}; the captain cannot \code{attack}.
A fighter that tried to \code{look} would be refused by the same gate that refuses a
mutation with no reason, and the refusal would be recorded in the intent-log --- the
permission is visible in the audit, not merely claimed in a comment.

\section{The fog is a semilattice, and the merge is its join}

The captain's state is a \code{FogMap}: for each cell, what the fleet knows of its
\emph{terrain}. The subtle design point is that terrain and \emph{occupancy} are
different kinds of fact and must not share a type.

Terrain in a static maze is permanent, so knowledge of it only accumulates. The four
values form a flat lattice with bottom $\bot = \mathtt{unknown}$ and top
$\top = \mathtt{conflict}$:
\[
  \mathtt{unknown} \;\sqsubseteq\; \{\mathtt{floor},\ \mathtt{wall},\ \mathtt{exit}\}
  \;\sqsubseteq\; \mathtt{conflict}.
\]
The three terrains are pairwise incomparable; the top exists only so the join is
\emph{total} --- two scouts reporting different terrain for one cell join to
$\mathtt{conflict}$, which on an honest static maze never happens (a tested
invariant). The cell join $\code{joinKnown}$ is commutative, associative,
idempotent, with $\mathtt{unknown}$ the identity and $\mathtt{conflict}$ absorbing;
these are checked exhaustively over the five values.

\code{mergeFog} lifts the join cellwise. It is the \textbf{amalgamation} the fleet is
built to demonstrate, and it is tested to be idempotent, commutative, associative,
and \emph{monotone} (merging only ever climbs the lattice --- knowledge never
decreases). This is the ``real algebra'' pole of the gold family: the treasury sums
sub-balances up a chart of accounts; \code{org-chart} joins partial knowledge up a
semilattice; the fleet does the latter. It is emphatically \emph{not} the
star-system captain's \code{tensorC}, which merely gathers two reports into a tuple.

\begin{keybox}[Why occupancy is not in the lattice]
A monster \emph{dies} when the fighter clears it, so a cell's occupancy can retract
--- it is not monotone. Folding ``monster'' into the terrain enum (as a first draft
did) would make a kill drop a cell $\mathtt{monster} \to \mathtt{floor}$ and the
monotonicity claim would be a lie. So monsters live in a separate, revisable
dictionary, and \code{mergeSighting} \emph{retracts} a monster at a cell it now
observes to be empty. Terrain joins monotonically; occupancy tracks the latest look.
\end{keybox}

\section{The round is a dependent pair}

The captain's control loop composes the scout and the fighter with sequential
composition $\lhd$ --- the dependent pair --- using the vendored container
combinators in \code{lib/algebra.ts}. One round:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=TS]
seqC(scout, fighter).step(
  { tag: "explore" },                      // control DOWN: dispatch the scout
  (scoutReport) => {                        // the scout's position chooses the next prompt
    for (const s of scoutReport.sightings)  // data UP: amalgamate into the fog ...
      captainFog = mergeSighting(captainFog, s);   // ... this is the M1 join
    return scoutReport.blockedBy             // control DOWN again
      ? { tag: "engage", target: scoutReport.blockedBy.monsterId,
          path: pathOverFog(captainFog, fighterPos, adjacentTo(scoutReport.blockedBy.at)) }
      : { tag: "standby" };
  });
\end{lstlisting}

Read it as the type. $\lhd$ is the dependent pair $(p : P)\,\otimes\,(T(u\,p) \to
R\,p)$: the scout's report is the value on which the fighter's prompt depends. The
fighter's order cannot be \emph{built} until the scout's report exists --- that
dependency is exactly why the composition must be $\lhd$ and not $\otimes$. The
star-system captain's \code{tensorC(science, engineering)} owes both replies and
lets neither depend on the other; here the whole point is that the second prompt is a
function of the first answer. The amalgamation --- the semilattice join of
\textsection 3 --- is the body of that function. Control flows down, data flows up,
state (the fog) is threaded through the captain across rounds.

\begin{keybox}[A soundness trap, avoided]
The fighter's shape is a sum: \code{engage} or \code{standby}. Declaring the
fighter's container as a \emph{union of two fibres} would let TypeScript's
conditional types distribute $\lhd$ over $+$, silently computing
$(\text{scout} \lhd \text{engage}) + (\text{scout} \lhd \text{standby})$ --- ``decide
then ask'' --- when what the round needs is $\text{scout} \lhd (\text{engage} +
\text{standby})$, ``ask then decide.'' The fix is to give the fighter a \emph{single}
fibre with a union \emph{shape}, \code{Fib<FighterShape, FighterReport>}, so the pair
stays a single dependent step.
\end{keybox}

\section{Existential hiding, literally}

The captain is never handed the world. Its only state is the fog; its only input is
scout reports. It explores --- repeating the round --- until a route to the exit
exists \emph{on what it already knows}, then it marches. Because it halts the instant
the exit becomes reachable, large regions it never needed stay dark. On the shipped
$21\times15$ maze (a perfect maze: one path between any two cells, hence ten dead
ends that force the scout to backtrack), the captain reaches the exit with
\textbf{136 of 315 cells still \code{unknown}} on its map. The closing screen of the
demo prints the captain's final fog beside the true maze; the contrast is the point.

The scout, correspondingly, is a worker whose store --- its own fog --- is hidden.
Within one \code{explore} dispatch it looks, steps toward the nearest frontier, and
repeats up to five times, stopping early if its next step would be a monster (it
reports \code{blockedBy} and holds position: ``retreat'' is handing control back, not
walking home). The captain sees only the sightings it chose to report, never the
scout's working map. The fighter, blind, cannot even see where it is: it
\emph{dead-reckons} its position from the orders it has executed, and reads its own
hit points back from each attack's committed effect --- the one thing a blind unit
can still learn.

\section{Two logs, three actors}

Nothing about the audit changed; it simply now spans three agents. Every move and
attack goes through the same gate as the single-agent maze: the registry is checked,
a non-empty reason is required before the command runs, a write-ahead record is
appended to the intent-log (Layer~2, the \emph{why}) with a correlation id, and on
commit the \code{dungeon} CLI appends to the ship's log (Layer~1, the \emph{what},
no reason). The acting unit is recorded in both, so \code{agent:scout},
\code{agent:fighter}, and \code{agent:captain} appear as distinct actors.
Reconciliation cross-checks the two logs: an alarm is a committed mutation with no
logged reason (it must never exist); a blocked action --- a refusal --- is visible
only in Layer~2. On the shipped run, reconciliation reports all mutations matched and
zero alarms: the captain moved the world only through reasoned, logged orders, and
only ever onto ground a scout had revealed.

\section{Determinism, and the replay}

Every seam is seeded in the scripted path: the scout's and captain's searches
explore neighbours in a fixed order, ids and the clock are injected stand-ins, and
there is no randomness or wall-clock anywhere in the agents. \code{verify.sh} runs
the fleet demo twice and diffs stdout, both logs, the move trace, \emph{and} the
generated website. The demo writes three artifacts: \code{moves.txt} (the moves as
plain text), \code{trace.json} (every frame: the true positions and the captain's
fog), and a self-contained \code{index.html} that animates the solve --- the true
maze with the captain's fog lifting over it as the scout reports come in, the fighter
clearing monsters, and the captain marching out at the end. The website is a pure
function of the deterministic trace, so it too is byte-identical across runs.

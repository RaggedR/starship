THE STARSHIP — what we are modelling, in plain English
Version 0.1 — 2026-10-29

Picture a single ship sitting in a small star system. The ship has a state —
how much fuel is in the tanks, how much hull is left, where it is and which way
it is pointing, how its limited power is split between shields and weapons and
engines, and what is in the cargo hold. That state is the whole world. Nothing
happens to it except through commands, and every command that changes it gets
written into the ship's log along with the reason it was given.

On the bridge sit five people, and the sharp part is that each one is allowed to
touch only their own instruments.

  - The science officer can look but not act. They can run a sensor sweep, read
    the ship's status, check the manifest — and that is all. They cannot steer
    or fire. Their job is to look out the window and say what they see.

  - The helm officer can steer. They can burn fuel to change course and dock at
    a station. They cannot fire the weapons. If they ask for a burn that needs
    more fuel than the tanks hold, the ship simply refuses — the burn never
    happens.

  - The weapons officer can fire, and nothing else. They cannot move the ship.
    And they cannot fire on their own say-so — firing needs the captain's
    authorization first.

  - The engineering officer manages resources. They shift power between shields,
    weapons and engines, and move cargo. They cannot exceed the power budget, so
    they cannot route more than the ship actually has.

  - The captain gives no readings and flies nothing directly. The captain
    delegates — asks the science officer for an assessment, asks the helm
    officer for an escape course, gathers those answers into one picture of the
    situation, and then decides and issues the orders, each with a stated
    reason.

Now a scene. A hazard appears. The captain tells science "tell me what is out
there," and science reports back "a mine field two thousand kilometres off the
port bow." The captain tells helm "plot me a burn that clears it," and helm
reports "a burn along this vector, it will cost forty units of fuel, we have
sixty." The captain weighs the two reports, decides to run, and orders the burn
with the reason "evading the mine field reported off the port bow." The burn
commits, the fuel drops, and the log records what was done and why. If instead
helm had proposed a burn costing eighty units, the ship would have refused it,
and from the captain's chair a refused burn and a broken engine look exactly the
same — all you know is the ship did not move.

And it scales up. A flagship captain commands a whole fleet, and delegates not
to officers but to the captains of the other ships, each of whom runs their own
bridge below. The flagship never sees inside another ship. It only hears each
ship's status report come back up. A fleet is just ships nested inside a ship.

The shape underneath. There is a world of state, a set of workers who may each
touch only their own slice of it, a boss who gathers their reports and issues
the orders that actually change things, a hard rule that quietly refuses
anything illegal, and a permanent record of every change and the reason for it.
The starship is the version that makes the delegation and the who-may-touch-what
vivid. Its sibling, the treasury, is the version that makes the adding-up
honest. See the sibling showpiece at https://github.com/RaggedR/treasury.

The shape itself — a worker as a container morphism, the boss who gathers and
decides, the gate that quietly refuses a denied command the same way a broken
tool fails — is MacBeth's. It is set down in Note 5, "The toy harness is a
worker" (~/git/harness/toy-harness-is-a-worker.pdf), which MacBeth sent in
